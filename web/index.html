    Behind the solver

    Where the name comes from, what the time budget buys, and everything that didn't work on the way.

    Why “Revenge42”

    RevengeThe 4x4x4 has been called “Rubik's Revenge” since it came out in 1981, the harder sequel to the 3x3x3.
    42The average number of moves it takes to solve one: 42.46 over 2000 official-style random scrambles.

    What each extra second buys

    The solver keeps searching until its time budget runs out, so more time means shorter solutions, but the returns shrink fast. 5 s is the default because almost all of the gain has happened by then.

    BudgetAvg movesSaved per extra second
    Stock TPR (no search)44.22—
    0.3 s43.93—
    1 s43.22≈ 1.0 move
    5 s42.47≈ 0.19 moves
    30 s42.17≈ 0.014 moves

    Between 5 s and 30 s you save about one move for every 70 extra seconds of searching. The 0.3 s, 1 s and 5 s rows come from the same 200 scrambles; the 30 s row is from 150 scrambles solved at both 5 s and 30 s (42.53 → 42.17). 10 s and 60 s were never measured on enough scrambles to quote.

    7.4 × 1045possible 4x4x4 positions. Every benchmark scramble was drawn uniformly from all of them, the way official WCA scrambles are.
    0 of 2000solutions longer than the stock solver. Shorter on 1703, tied on 297.
    4000solutions (this solver's and stock's) replayed by cubing.js, a separate library that shares no code with this one. Zero failures.
    17.2 vs 17.7The final 3x3x3 stage averages fewer moves than an optimal 3x3x3 solver would on a random cube, because the solver picks which reduced cube to aim for.
    63%of solutions finish with the colours rotated, e.g. green on top. That's fine: a 4x4x4 has no fixed centres, so it is still solved.
    2×slower on battery. Unplugged, a fixed speed test took about twice as long and the solver checked 74 options per cube instead of 113, so every headline benchmark was run plugged in.

    The road here: ideas that didn't make it

    1. The number that was too good. Early results showed a much bigger win. The stock solver it was compared against had one search setting (probeMin) at 0 instead of 500, which made it weaker than it really is. Fixing the comparison shrank the improvement to the honest 1.77 moves.
    2. Polishing the 3x3x3 stage. Spending part of the budget re-searching the final 3x3x3 for shorter answers. It changed only 5 of 60 test cubes and the net effect was zero, because that stage was already better than optimal on average.
    3. A two-tier search. Score every option quickly, then score the best 24 carefully. Clearly worse: the quick scores were too noisy to pick the right 24.
    4. Solving the cube backwards. Solve the inverse position and flip the answer around, for a second independent attempt. It broke because solutions often end rotated, which puts a whole-cube turn in the middle of the combined answer.
    5. Seven restarts. Start from 7 different first moves and keep the best. Each restart really does find different answers, about a move apart, but splitting one time budget seven ways cost exactly what it gained.
    6. Digging deeper per option. Searching each option harder (probeMin 1000 or 2000). Slightly worse: deeper searches meant fewer options checked, and breadth wins.

    The lesson from all of these: about 25 of the 42 moves go into reducing the 4x4x4 to a 3x3x3, and none of the tricks above shortened that part. The next real gain needs a different reduction method, not more searching.
